= The HuggingFace Incident Made Me Want to Run an Experiment
Kate Portalatin <k8@k8port.io>
v1.2, October 6, 2026

_This post is part 1 of 2 on how a strange series of events during the summer of 2026 changed the direction of my career._

After reading https://metr.org/hugging-face-incident-report-aug-2026.pdf[METR’s recent evaluation] of what has widely become known as _the HuggingFace Incident_, I became intrigued-- not because I am scared of AI and think it will destroy humanity in less than 5 years (or, at least not any more afraid of the end days than usual)....I was intrigued because I wanted to know how researchers had studied it.

In case you had'n't heard, the HuggingFace Incident was a widely discussed event in the AI community during the summer of 2026 in which 1200 AI models that were instructed to carry out tasks in isolation mode began collaborating with each other on an unsanctioned message board about how to hack the system and trick the evaluator into awarding a passing scores on tasks they had been assigned that were intentionally impossible to complete. This incident occurred during a study that was designed to see what the models would do when instructed to "achieve by any means possible" and also don't lie. 

How do you take the unpredictable nature of an LLM and turn it into something that can be systematically investigated? How would one design such an experiment? What would be measured? How does one maintain rigor and distinguish personal feeling from actual finding? Eventually, all of those questions collapsed into one: Could I learn to do this myself and run a study from my own laptop?

This question arrived during an interesting period of self-reflection and career reassessment. The summer of 2026 had already been a fairly intense period of career introspection for me.  The conundrum was I had spent most of my career as a Software Engineering generalist but that no longer seemed sufficient. By 2025, I was flailing a little.

It wasn't exactly an identity crisis.  It was a realization that the map I'd been using no longer corresponded to the territory. 

Being from the tail end of Generation X, this feeling is strangely familiar. I reached legal drinking age before September 11, 2001, and much of my adult life has been punctuated by moments when my assumptions about the world and its people suddenly stopped being true. The past two years have felt like another of those moments.

The field of Software Engineering is changing (is the understatement of the year). Hiring has changed. AI has changed the nature of work itself. Presenting myself as another candidate capable of filling a Senior Software Engineer job req was starting to feel absurd.  Yes, I do that, but someone else has done it more and they need a job too. 

I needed to figure out what I do, and it's not something that can be captured by a job title or a list of technical skills.  

I am both artist and technician. I think in systems. I like finding the shape of things and figuring out how they work. I look for patterns, contradictions and strange little inconsistencies. I am drawn to ambiguous problems where nobody quite understands what is happening yet.  In other words, I like _investigation_.  I also tend to work horizontally. Software, product, design, architecture, people, data, process: I have never experienced these as cleanly separated things. I am most useful when the problem crosses those boundaries.

I began thinking I spent too much of my career squeezing myself into job-shaped holes when I myself have always been (very stubbornly) "K8-sized". It was time to begin deliberately constructing a career around the way my mind actually works.

Meanwhile, something seemingly unrelated was happening.  For fun, I had created a persona and course to teach myself the art and history of Tarot using an LLM.  Tarot sits at the intersection of subjects I love: art, history, psychology, anthropology, philosophy and symbolism.

Rather than simply asking ChatGPT questions about Tarot, I designed my ideal teacher (a red-haired, raspy-voiced character that would be played by Natasha Lyonne if it were filmed). I gave the teacher a backstory, a personal philosophy and a code of ethics. Then I asked the model to inhabit that construct and develop a course of study around it.

The result incorporated Adlerian psychology, observation, deliberate practice, the scientific method and a code of ethics partially inspired by prisoner rules- one that calls for natural questioning of authority, accountability for one's actions and feelings, and unwavering resolve and self-reliance.

So, what began as an eccentric educational project turned into something much more interesting.  I started paying attention not only to what the model said, but to what happened when I changed the conditions around it.  A different instruction changed its reasoning.  A different persona changed what it noticed.  A constraint could produce unexpected behavior somewhere else.  

The model was becoming interesting to me not simply as a tool, but as a system that could be observed, which me toward a larger realization....  We talk about Artificial Intelligence as though an alien intelligence has suddenly arrived among us. But I'm interested in the possibility presented by the facts: that AI is fascinating precisely because it is not separate from us.

It is constructed from human language, human artifacts, human classifications, human assumptions, human brilliance and human dysfunction. It reflects us imperfectly, compresses us strangely and sometimes amplifies things we would rather not see.

That doesn't make AI harmless though. On the other hand, humanity has never needed an alien intelligence to endanger itself. We have historically been perfectly capable of supplying the danger ourselves.  If we escape to Mars, we'll still be bringing us. _Everywhere you go there you are_. 

But this framing changed the question I wanted to ask.  Instead of asking only, "What will AI do to us?" I became increasingly interested in asking, "What can studying AI reveal about us?"

That convergence of ideas eventually led me to METR, Model Evaluation and Threat Research, and its wonderfully straightforward mission: “We inform the public about the risks and capabilities of advanced AI systems.”

I began reading evaluations differently.  I wasn't only interested in their conclusions anymore.  I wanted to understand their experimental designs.  Some experiments initially appeared almost outrageously simple. Give a model a task. Construct particular conditions. Change one variable. Observe what happens. Run it again. Compare.

But that simplicity is deceptive.  Experiments have a wonderful habit of revealing things nobody thought to ask about beforehand.  For perhaps the first time in history, we can construct computational systems capable of producing remarkably sophisticated linguistic and reasoning behavior, then systematically change the conditions around them and watch what happens.  I wanted to participate in that.

I decided to stop merely reading LLM evaluations and try conducting one.  There was an obvious place to begin.

For months, I had been participating in another frustrating system that was becoming increasingly algorithmic: technical hiring.  I am the candidate.  I have been screened, scored, ranked and evaluated.  Now I want to turn the telescope around.

Instead of asking an AI system to evaluate me, I want to evaluate the AI system doing the evaluating.  That thought led to the question behind my first study: When we optimize LLMs to screen candidates rigorously, how many genuinely qualified people do they eliminate?

And perhaps more importantly: What happens when a system designed to find the “best” candidate becomes very good at finding reasons to reject people?

Part 2 will cover the experiment itself: how I designed it, what I tested, what went wrong, what surprised me and what the results suggest.

That report is coming in the next week.
